## Prominence research - 2026-10-21
The designer asked to identify the three most famous Inuit constellations independently
of the existing selection, allowing alternatives. No authoritative ranked top-three list
or popularity data was found. Names and traditions vary by community. The following is a
source-based shortlist, not a measured popularity ranking or a newly approved selection.

- **Aagjuuk:** strongest explicit evidence of broad cultural importance among the candidates.
  [Uqalurait, Astronomy](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt815m6) identifies Altair and Tarazed
  and describes the pair as one of the most important seasonal markers, used to time
  departures for hunting. [IPS 2000 proceedings](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.ips-planetarium.org/resource/resmgr/pdf-conf/2000_proc_Montreal-LoRes.pdf)
  also explicitly describes Aagjuuk as the most important and widely recognized constellation
  in its account of MacDonald's work with Igloolik elders; do not generalize this into a
  formal universal ranking. The adopted two-star variant remains the existing candidate.
- **Tukturjuit:** [Pinnguaq's own educational material](https://pinnguaq.com/learn/constellation-and-stellar-knowledge/)
  identifies seven Big Dipper stars as caribou and describes their use in orienting/navigation.
  It also appears in the [Igloolik Oral History Project's star resource](https://www.anijaarniq.com/stars),
  which presents stars and constellations used most often by Iglulingmiut. This supports
  prominence and documentation, not a numerical popularity rank.
- **Ullaktut:** the same oral-history resource gives elder testimony about its seasonal
  position and specifically features Suzanne Niviattian's telling of its story. The UAF
  museum's Cultural Star Knowledge sheet and WAG's INUA artist account also feature it.
  The familiar three belt stars and associated runners/hunters story support its value
  as a clear introduction. No source verified that it ranks third across all Inuit regions.

Other prominent sky figures were considered, including Nanurjuk (Aldebaran) and
Nuuttuittuq (Polaris). Both are single stars in the cited Igloolik account, so distinguish
them from multi-star groups; their prominence is not a reason to call either a constellation.
The oral-history source notes that Polaris's high altitude makes other stars preferable
for some Igloolik hunters. Do not assume Western North Star prominence applies uniformly.

Recommendation for discussion: keep Aagjuuk, Tukturjuit and Ullaktut as a well-documented
introductory trio with distinct candidate counts (2, 7, 3). No order, dates or answer
changed; user selection remains open. Source-dependent star membership and spelling must
be checked against the final community-specific version before art production.

## Nearby groups around Tukturjuit - 2026-10-21
User asked about other Inuit constellations near Tukturjuit. No replacement selected.
Checked the official Stellarium Inuit skyculture dataset, an adaptation of MacDonald's
research, against the existing HYG positions. Two useful closer groups in that documented
selection are Sivulliik (Arcturus and Muphrid, 2 stars) and Quturjuuk (Capella, Menkalinan,
Castor and Pollux, 4 stars). Their nearest member-star distances from any of the seven
Tukturjuit stars are approximately 31 and 43 degrees respectively, computed using great-
circle separation of catalogue RA/declination. These are nearest-member distances,
not constellation-centre distances or a claim of an exhaustive Inuit grouping catalogue.
For comparison, the selected Aagjuuk and Ullaktut groups are about 82 and 86 degrees away
by the same measure. Thus Tukturjuit/Sivulliik/Quturjuuk is an option for a tighter chart;
no new trio, final counts/code, artwork or rule adopted.

Nuuttuittuq (Polaris) is also a useful nearby reference but is one star, not a multi-star
constellation. The Igloolik Oral History Project describes its role separately.

## First Nations options allowed - 2026-10-21
Designer: "we don't have to stick to Inuit, we can use First Nations constellations as
well, they might be more numerous." Expanded research beyond the previously Inuit-only
selection. No claim verified that one tradition has more constellations than another.

Useful source bases:
- Wilfred Buck's own [Ininiw Acakosuk article](https://canadiangeographic.ca/articles/ininiw-acakosuk-the-cree-stars/)
  presents 16 Ininiw constellations on its map. It describes Mista Muskwa (Big Bear)
  with the Big Dipper and Tepakoop Pinesisuk (Seven Birds) with Corona Borealis.
- [MFNERC First Nations Astronomy](https://mfnerc.org/learning-hub/first-nations-astronomy/)
  provides a Wilfred Buck presentation as further primary teaching material.
- [Native Skywatchers' Ojibwe booklet](https://www.nativeskywatchers.com/articles/Booklet-Ojibwe-10-23-20-v9-screen-version.pdf)
  and its research table identify Ojiig/Fisher with the Big Dipper, Maang/Loon with
  the Little Dipper, and Ajiijaak/Crane with Cygnus. These are research candidates,
  not new selected chart geometry or validated puzzle star counts.

Assistant recommendation: select groups that fit one compact chart, have clear bounded
star membership and varied single-digit counts, and can be matched by distinct cutouts.
Attribute each name/story to its source nation and version. The same Western star group
can carry different Indigenous interpretations; those are alternative identifications,
not additional separate patterns to count on the chart. No new three-group selection,
star counts, chronology, answer or artwork adopted in this discussion.


## Mooz, Ajiijaak and Maang counts - 2026-10-21
User asked the counts in these Ojibwe constellations. No replacement selected yet.
- Maang: 7, explicitly stated in Annette Lee's published Ojibwe/Dakota sky-map research
  (https://escholarship.org/content/qt58m4f9pq/qt58m4f9pq.pdf) and Zhaawano Giizhik's
  own account of Maang (https://www.zhaawanart.com/post/ojibwe-anishinaabe-stars-planets-part-2-j-to-z).
- Ajiijaak: 9, explicitly described as a nine-star constellation in Zhaawano Giizhik's
  own Ojibwe star-map account (https://www.zhaawanart.com/post/star-stories-part-9-ojibwe-star-map).
- Mooz: no single total stated in the Native Skywatchers fall booklet. RASC World
  Asterisms Project handbook v2024.4, pp.1026-1027, describes a basic outline yielding
  14 distinct stars: 4 Great Square stars + zeta Pegasi + lambda/kappa Pegasi + eta
  Pegasi + 6/2/5/4/alpha/beta Lacertae. This 14 is our count of that particular described
  outline, not a universal cultural count or a verified total for all illustrated variants.
  The Native Skywatchers booklet also points out bell and heart stars beyond its body,
  legs, head and antlers. Its body alone is explicitly 4 stars. Before making a puzzle,
  select the exact star membership/outline. Do not silently label the 4-star body as
  the whole Mooz, or call the full booklet illustration 14 without verification.
