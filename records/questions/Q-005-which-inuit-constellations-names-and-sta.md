Inuit sky knowledge only (not a mix of nations), for the North connection.
Candidates from the 2026-09-27 brainstorm, all to check against sources before use:
- Tukturjuit, "the caribou" (the stars of the Big Dipper)
- Ullaktut, "the runners" (Orion's belt, hunters chasing a bear)
- Aagjuuk (Altair and Tarazed), whose return at dawn in midwinter meant the sun was coming back

Main source to check: *The Arctic Sky: Inuit Astronomy, Star Lore, and Legend*, John MacDonald (1998).
Needed for PZ-002: three constellations, their names and meanings, and how many stars each has as Inuit see them.


## Prominence research - 2026-10-21
The designer asked to identify the three most famous Inuit constellations independently
of the existing selection, allowing alternatives. No authoritative ranked top-three list
or popularity data was found. Names and traditions vary by community. The following is a
source-based shortlist, not a measured popularity ranking or a newly approved selection.

- **Aagjuuk:** strongest explicit evidence of broad cultural importance among the candidates.
  [Uqalurait, Astronomy](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt815m6) identifies Altair and Tarazed
  and describes the pair as one of the most important seasonal markers, used to time
  departures for hunting. [IPS 2000 proceedings](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.ips-planetarium.org/resource/resmgr/pdf-conf/2000_proc_Montreal-LoRes.pdf)
  also explicitly describes Aagjuuk as the most important and widely recognized constellation
  in its account of MacDonald's work with Igloolik elders; do not generalize this into a
  formal universal ranking. The adopted two-star variant remains the existing candidate.
- **Tukturjuit:** [Pinnguaq's own educational material](https://pinnguaq.com/learn/constellation-and-stellar-knowledge/)
  identifies seven Big Dipper stars as caribou and describes their use in orienting/navigation.
  It also appears in the [Igloolik Oral History Project's star resource](https://www.anijaarniq.com/stars),
  which presents stars and constellations used most often by Iglulingmiut. This supports
  prominence and documentation, not a numerical popularity rank.
- **Ullaktut:** the same oral-history resource gives elder testimony about its seasonal
  position and specifically features Suzanne Niviattian's telling of its story. The UAF
  museum's Cultural Star Knowledge sheet and WAG's INUA artist account also feature it.
  The familiar three belt stars and associated runners/hunters story support its value
  as a clear introduction. No source verified that it ranks third across all Inuit regions.

Other prominent sky figures were considered, including Nanurjuk (Aldebaran) and
Nuuttuittuq (Polaris). Both are single stars in the cited Igloolik account, so distinguish
them from multi-star groups; their prominence is not a reason to call either a constellation.
The oral-history source notes that Polaris's high altitude makes other stars preferable
for some Igloolik hunters. Do not assume Western North Star prominence applies uniformly.

Recommendation for discussion: keep Aagjuuk, Tukturjuit and Ullaktut as a well-documented
introductory trio with distinct candidate counts (2, 7, 3). No order, dates or answer
changed; user selection remains open. Source-dependent star membership and spelling must
be checked against the final community-specific version before art production.
